Before I studied products,
I studied stories.

A good question can change
the whole story.

Documentary filmmaking taught me to stay curious when someone’s experience did not fit the story I expected. That habit still guides my research: listen carefully, look for the exception, and be willing to change the question.

My background spans communication, learning design, and UX research. In the Oak work, those interests meet in practical questions: does a requested feature address the barrier people described? Does an explanation of privacy answer a concern about control? What evidence would change the team’s next step? I like making those questions specific enough to investigate together.

I work across product research and design, and volunteer as a UX Research Lead at Oppia. Away from work, I run tabletop games. Facilitation, pacing, and leaving room for quieter voices matter there too. It is another place where listening changes what happens next.

01

Understand the reported issue

Ask what happened, what the person expected and what evidence is available. Keep the affected task and the open questions attached to the finding.

02

Connect people to the detail

Explain the problem in language the audience can use. Work with technical colleagues to distinguish a known constraint from an assumption that needs checking.

03

Keep the handoff useful

Document the recommendation, its rationale and the next check. Preserve enough context for a colleague to pick up the work without repeating the investigation.

PROPOSED APPROACH FOR AFFIRMEDRX

A status record should answer the next question.

I would start by learning how your patient advocates, client managers and Data Operations team handle a reported issue. These are the questions I would use to review one case with them.

  1. What needs attention?

    Capture the reported problem, the affected client or file, relevant dates and the evidence needed to scope it. Follow the team’s privacy and access rules.

  2. What is confirmed?

    Work with the appropriate technical specialist to separate the known impact from an untested cause or an open question.

  3. Who owns the next step?

    Keep the owner, current status, next action and next update time in the shared record. Give business teams the detail they need to help the person waiting.

  4. What closes the loop?

    Confirm the resolution with the responsible team, record what changed and flag recurring issues for a process review.

Proposal for discussion, based on the role description. My experience is in product research and workflow design; pharmacy-benefit data and AffirmedRx’s operating processes would be new domains to learn.
